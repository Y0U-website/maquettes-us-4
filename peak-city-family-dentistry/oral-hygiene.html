Oral Hygiene

Call Us Book an Appointment Why Oral Hygiene Matters at Every Age Maintaining a consistent oral hygiene routine is one of the most important things you can do for your long-term health. Adults lose more teeth to gum disease than to any other single cause—and research suggests that three out of four people will experience some form of periodontal disease during their lifetime. The encouraging news is that most of it is preventable with proper brushing and flossing, performed every day without exception.

Both tooth decay and gum disease are triggered by bacterial plaque—a soft, sticky film that builds up on your teeth around the gumline throughout the day. Removing this film consistently before it hardens into tartar is the foundation of good oral health.

How to Brush Effectively

If you experience any pain while brushing or have questions about technique, call our Apex office at 919-362-8797 and we’ll be glad to help.

Dr. Viau recommends a soft-bristled toothbrush. Hold it so the bristles rest at a 45-degree angle against the area where your gums and teeth meet. Using light, circular strokes—small enough to cover one or two teeth at a time—work gently along the outer surfaces of every tooth. Avoid pressing so hard that you feel discomfort; the goal is to disrupt the plaque film, not to scrub aggressively.

Once you have finished the outer surfaces, use the same small circular motion on the inner surfaces of your back teeth. For the inner surfaces of your upper and lower front teeth, hold the brush upright and make short back-and-forth passes over each tooth, brushing gently into the surrounding gum tissue as you go.

Finish by cleaning the chewing surfaces of all teeth with short, controlled strokes. Reposition the brush frequently so you reach every surface. A mirror is helpful for confirming full coverage. When you are done, rinse thoroughly to flush away any loosened plaque.

How to Floss Correctly

Gum disease most often begins in the spaces between teeth—areas your toothbrush can never fully reach. Flossing is the most reliable method for removing plaque from these surfaces, and while learning proper technique takes a little practice, it becomes second nature quickly.

Start with about 18 inches of floss (waxed tends to slide more easily). Wind most of it loosely around one middle finger and the remaining portion around the middle finger of your other hand, leaving a short working section between them.

For upper teeth, hold the floss firmly between your thumbs and index fingers. Ease it between teeth using a gentle back-and-forth motion—never snap it. Once the floss reaches the gumline, curve it into a C around one tooth and slide it carefully into the groove between the tooth and gum until you sense mild resistance. Glide the floss up and down along the tooth surface several times, then curve it around the adjacent tooth and repeat. Work your way across all upper teeth, shifting to a clean section of floss as it becomes soiled.

For lower teeth, guide the floss using your index fingers. Don’t overlook the back surface of the very last tooth on each side—top and bottom.

When finished, rinse well with water. Some bleeding and tenderness during the first week of regular flossing is normal. If your gums remain sore, you may be applying too much pressure. As plaque is removed consistently over several days, healthy gums will stop bleeding. If bleeding continues beyond two weeks, let us know.

Managing Sensitivity After Dental Treatment

It is common for teeth to feel more sensitive to temperature changes following dental work. This sensitivity should ease within a relatively short period, provided the area is kept clean. Neglecting oral hygiene around a treated tooth can extend or worsen sensitivity. If your teeth remain particularly sensitive, mention it to Dr. Viau at your next visit—a prescription-strength toothpaste or rinse formulated for sensitive teeth may help.

Selecting Oral Hygiene Products

The dental aisle can feel overwhelming. Here are some straightforward guidelines to help you choose well.

Electric and sonic toothbrushes are a sound investment for most people. They are safe, effective, and many patients find them easier to use correctly than manual brushes. Water irrigators rinse debris from around the gumline but do not replace flossing—use them as a supplement, not a substitute. If you use an interproximal (small interdental) brush to clean between teeth, ask our team to show you the correct technique to avoid injuring your gums.

Fluoride toothpastes and mouth rinses, when used alongside daily brushing and flossing, can reduce the rate of new cavities significantly. Note that fluoride rinses are not appropriate for children under six. Tartar-control toothpastes are effective at slowing tartar formation above the gumline, but since gum disease begins below it, these products alone will not prevent early periodontal disease. Anti-plaque rinses bearing an ADA Seal of Acceptance may provide additional benefit as part of a complete routine.

Why Professional Cleanings Still Matter

Even the most thorough daily brushing and flossing will leave some calculus buildup in hard-to-reach spots over time. A professional cleaning removes calculus from every surface—including those your toothbrush and floss cannot reach—and gives your hygienist the opportunity to catch any early changes before they become problems. Regular cleanings, combined with your daily home care routine, are the most effective strategy for keeping your teeth for life.
