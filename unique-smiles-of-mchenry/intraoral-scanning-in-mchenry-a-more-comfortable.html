How Is Intraoral Scanning Different From Traditional Dental Impressions?

Traditional impressions typically use trays filled with a soft material that records the shape of the teeth as it sets. Traditional methods have been used successfully for many years and may still be appropriate in some situations.

Intraoral scanning takes a different approach. Instead of creating an immediate physical mold, the scanner produces a digital model.

For many patients, avoiding a tray full of impression material can make the experience more comfortable. This can be especially helpful for people with a sensitive gag reflex or those who dislike the feeling of impression material in the mouth.

Digital scanning can also make it easier to correct a small incomplete area. If part of a conventional impression is unusable, a new impression may sometimes be necessary. With intraoral scanning, the dental professional may be able to rescan only the area that needs more information.

Neither method should be viewed as automatically right for every clinical situation. The dentist selects the approach that provides suitable information for the planned treatment.

Benefits of Intraoral Scanning

Intraoral scanning offers several advantages for patients and dental teams when digital impressions are appropriate. Potential benefits include:

Intraoral scanning does not eliminate the importance of clinical skill. Instead, it provides dentists with another tool for collecting and communicating detailed information.

  • Improved comfort: Many patients prefer a small scanning wand to trays filled with impression material.
  • Reduced gagging concerns: Intraoral scanning may be easier for patients with a sensitive gag reflex.
  • Immediate visualization: Digital models can often be reviewed on a screen during the appointment.
  • Efficient corrections: Specific incomplete areas can often be rescanned without repeating the entire record.
  • Detailed three-dimensional information: The digital model shows the shape and position of teeth from multiple angles.
  • Digital transfer: Records can be sent electronically when needed for treatment planning or laboratory fabrication.
  • Useful patient education: Seeing a digital model may help patients understand areas being discussed by the dentist.
  • Support for multiple treatments: Digital scans can assist with restorative, orthodontic, implant, and appliance planning.

Can You See Your Digital Scan?

One of the interesting features of intraoral scanning is the ability to display the model on a monitor. Patients may be able to see their teeth from angles that are difficult to view in a mirror.

For example, a dentist can enlarge a tooth, rotate the digital model, or point out areas related to the treatment plan. This visual information can make explanations easier to follow.

However, a digital model should not be interpreted without professional context. A patient may see an uneven area or unusual shape and assume there is a problem when it may simply be normal anatomy.

Therefore, the value of viewing the scan comes from combining the image with the dentist’s explanation and other examination findings.

Does Intraoral Scanning Hurt?

Intraoral scanning is noninvasive and does not involve cutting or drilling. Patients may feel the scanner moving around the teeth and cheeks, but the scan itself should not cause tooth pain.

Some people may become tired from keeping their mouth open, particularly if a large area needs to be captured. Meanwhile, certain back teeth can require careful positioning of the scanner.

If you have a sensitive gag reflex, jaw discomfort, or difficulty opening widely, tell the dental team before scanning begins. They can often adjust positioning, take brief pauses, or work in smaller sections to make the process more manageable.

How Can Intraoral Scanning Help With Dental Crowns?

A crown must fit over a prepared tooth while also contacting neighboring teeth and working properly with the opposing bite. Therefore, accurate records are an important part of crown fabrication.

After the tooth is prepared, intraoral scanning can capture the tooth and surrounding area. The digital file can then be used as part of the design and fabrication process.

The dentist reviews the scan carefully because accuracy depends on capturing all relevant margins and surfaces. If part of the preparation is not visible, additional scanning or tissue management may be required.

Once the final crown is returned or fabricated according to the treatment workflow, the dentist still checks its fit, contacts, appearance, and bite before placement. Intraoral scanning supports the process, but the final clinical evaluation remains essential.

How Does Digital Scanning Support Dental Bridge Planning?

Dental bridges involve several teeth working together, which makes accurate relationships especially important. A digital scan can record the supporting teeth, missing space, neighboring teeth, and opposing bite.

This information helps guide the creation of a bridge that fits the prepared teeth while also occupying the missing space appropriately.

In addition, digital records can help communicate details with the dental laboratory. The goal is a restoration that supports function, fits the patient’s bite, and complements surrounding teeth.

Because dental bridges must be cleaned and maintained after placement, scanning is only one step in the overall treatment. Oral hygiene, supporting tooth health, and regular dental visits remain equally important.

Can Intraoral Scanning Be Used for Invisalign?

Digital scanning technology is commonly associated with orthodontic planning because clear aligners require detailed information about tooth position. A digital model can help document how teeth are arranged before treatment.

For patients considering Invisalign , the treatment process may involve digital records that help with planning the sequence of tooth movement.

The scanner itself does not straighten teeth. Instead, it provides data that can be used within a larger orthodontic workflow.

A full evaluation is still necessary to determine whether clear aligner treatment is appropriate, because tooth movement depends on the bite, gums, bone support, spacing, crowding, and other clinical factors.
