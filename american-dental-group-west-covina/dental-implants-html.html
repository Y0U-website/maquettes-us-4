The process, step by step

A fuller walkthrough is on our implant procedure page.

  • Consultation and 3D imaging. A clinical exam plus a cone-beam scan showing bone volume, bone density and the position of nerves and sinuses. You leave with a written treatment plan and an itemised estimate.
  • Any groundwork first. Extraction of a failing tooth, periodontal treatment, or bone grafting where the site needs rebuilding. Some of this happens at the same appointment as the implant; some needs its own healing time.
  • Implant placement. A titanium post is placed into the planned position under local anaesthetic, with sedation available. Most patients are back to normal activity the next day.
  • Osseointegration. Over roughly three to six months the bone grows onto the implant surface and locks it in place. A temporary restoration covers the gap while that happens.
  • Abutment and final crown. Once the implant is confirmed stable, a connector is fitted and a custom crown is made to match the shade and shape of your own teeth, then adjusted against your bite.

If you need bone grafting first

Bone loss at the implant site is common and usually solvable. Graft material is placed into the defect and the body rebuilds bone in it over several months, after which the implant goes in normally. Small defects are often grafted at the same appointment as the implant. Recovery, and what the first weeks feel like

Most patients are surprised by how ordinary it is. Expect soreness and swelling for two to three days, managed with over-the-counter pain relief, and softer food for about a week. Strenuous exercise waits a few days. The longer part of recovery is invisible: the months of bone fusing to the implant, during which there is usually nothing to feel at all.

Dental Implants FAQ

How long do dental implants last?

The implant itself the titanium post in the bone is designed to be permanent, and studies consistently report success rates above 95 % at ten years. The crown on top is a separate part and behaves like any other restoration: expect 10 to 15 years before it needs replacing, sometimes longer. What decides the outcome is gum health and maintenance, not the implant brand.

Am I a candidate for dental implants?

Most adults with a missing tooth are. The three things that matter are enough bone to hold the implant, gums free of active periodontal disease, and general health good enough for a minor surgical procedure. Smoking, uncontrolled diabetes and untreated gum disease all lower success rates, and all three can be addressed before treatment rather than ruling you out. A 3D scan gives a definitive answer.

What if I don’t have enough bone?

That is common, because the jaw starts shrinking as soon as a tooth is lost. Bone grafting rebuilds the site so an implant can be placed, either as a separate procedure a few months ahead or at the same appointment as the implant when the defect is small. Dr. Hanasab’s periodontics training covers exactly this work, so grafting is done here rather than referred elsewhere.

Does getting a dental implant hurt?

Placing an implant is usually less uncomfortable than having the tooth out. The site is fully numbed, and sedation is available. Most patients take over-the-counter pain relief for a day or two and describe the soreness as similar to a bruise. Swelling settles within a few days.

How long does the whole process take?

Plan on three to six months from placement to final crown in a straightforward case, because the implant has to fuse to the bone before it can carry a bite. Bone grafting adds a few months. You are not without a tooth in the meantime a temporary restoration covers the gap while healing runs.

Are implants better than a bridge or a denture?

For a single missing tooth, an implant leaves the neighbouring teeth untouched, whereas a bridge requires cutting down two healthy teeth to carry it. An implant also loads the bone the way a natural root does, which is why the ridge holds its shape instead of shrinking under a denture. Bridges and dentures are still the right answer in some cases, and we will say so when they are.

Does insurance cover dental implants?

Coverage varies a lot. Many plans that once excluded implants entirely now contribute to parts of the treatment the extraction, the crown, or a share of the implant itself.
