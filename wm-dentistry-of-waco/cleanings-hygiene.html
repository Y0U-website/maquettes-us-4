Routine Maintenance vs. Catching Up After a Long Gap

Not every cleaning is the same visit with a different name on the calendar. A routine hygiene visit, the kind most patients come in for, pairs a professional cleaning with an exam . Your dentist looks at the whole picture while the hygienist works. That&#x27;s the most common appointment we see, and for a patient who&#x27;s kept up with regular visits, it&#x27;s usually straightforward.

But if it&#x27;s been a year, two years, longer - the visit looks different. Plaque and tartar don&#x27;t sit still, they build, harden, and push under the gumline where a quick polish won&#x27;t reach. That&#x27;s not a judgment, it&#x27;s just biology. So the cleaning needed to catch you back up takes more time. It also takes more attention to those areas than a standard maintenance visit would.

Here&#x27;s the honest answer to what drives cost up or down. Two things: whether you&#x27;re maintaining or catching up, and whether additional imaging is needed to check what&#x27;s beneath the surface. That second factor is what surprises people. If we can&#x27;t see it, we can&#x27;t tell you what&#x27;s going on down there, and guessing isn&#x27;t how we practice.

Either way, the visit still does the same core job. Removing buildup, polishing, and checking gum health, all in the same appointment. We&#x27;re conservative about it, too - cleanings are a checkpoint for catching small things early, not a chance to sell you something you don&#x27;t need. If there&#x27;s decay or gum disease that goes beyond routine maintenance, that gets its own plan, discussed separately, so you know exactly what you&#x27;re saying yes to and why.

When a Cleaning Isn&#x27;t Enough on Its Own

A routine cleaning is built for maintenance, not repair. If you&#x27;re keeping up with regular visits and your gums are healthy, that appointment is exactly what your mouth needs. Nothing more. But if it&#x27;s been a while, the visit can look different.

Here&#x27;s the thing we tell patients often: a cleaning treats plaque and tartar and gives us a clear picture of gum health. It doesn&#x27;t treat existing decay. It doesn&#x27;t treat gum disease that&#x27;s already taken hold. Those are handled as separate treatment plans, built around what we find, not folded into the same visit as an afterthought.

So what does that look like in practice? Say you come in after a couple years away from the dentist. Your gums bleed easily, there&#x27;s some tenderness, and probing shows deeper pockets than we&#x27;d like. That&#x27;s not a routine cleaning anymore, that&#x27;s a starting point for a real conversation about treatment.

  • Decay that shows up on an exam or additional imaging gets its own plan, not a quick fix during your cleaning appointment
  • Gum disease beyond early stages needs a different approach than standard maintenance
  • Patients returning after a long gap often need a more involved visit before we&#x27;re back to routine six-month care

What Happens Step by Step During Your Visit

Nothing about a cleaning visit should feel like a mystery. You sit down, we walk you through it, and the whole thing moves in a clear order every time.

Most patients are in and out faster than they expect. And if we spot something small, we&#x27;ll tell you it&#x27;s small, we won&#x27;t dress it up as urgent when it isn&#x27;t. That&#x27;s the conservative philosophy at work, a cleaning is a checkpoint, not a sales moment.

If care has lapsed a bit, the process still runs the same order, it just takes more time at the plaque and tartar step. One thing we won&#x27;t do is treat existing decay or gum disease during a routine cleaning. Those get their own separate plan, discussed on their own terms once what we&#x27;re dealing with.

  • Check-in and quick health update. We ask what&#x27;s changed since your last visit, any new sensitivity, any spots that bother you when you eat or brush.
  • Gum health evaluation. We look at your gum tissue first, not last. This tells us whether you&#x27;re dealing with a standard maintenance cleaning or something that needs a more involved approach.
  • Plaque and tartar removal. This is the core of the visit, clearing buildup from areas your toothbrush can&#x27;t reach on its own.
  • Polishing. Surfaces get smoothed and cleaned. This makes it harder for plaque to grab hold again right away.
  • Imaging, if needed. Not every visit calls for it. We only bring in imaging when there&#x27;s a reason to check what&#x27;s happening beneath the surface.
  • Review with Dr. Morgan. Before you leave, findings get reviewed so you know exactly what we saw and what, if anything, needs attention next.

How Often You Need a Cleaning

Six months. That&#x27;s the number most people have stuck in their head, and for a lot of adults it&#x27;s still the right answer. A routine hygiene visit twice a year gives our team a chance to remove plaque and tartar before it turns into something bigger. It also keeps gum health on our radar instead of an afterthought.

But six months isn&#x27;t a rule carved in stone, it&#x27;s a starting point. Some patients do better on a three or four month schedule. That&#x27;s especially true if they&#x27;ve had gum issues before or tend to build up tartar fast. We see this every week: two patients with nearly identical brushing habits. One needs more frequent visits just because of how their body handles plaque. Genetics and saliva chemistry play a bigger role than most people expect.

So how do you know which camp you&#x27;re in? Ask yourself if your gums bleed when you floss, or if your last cleaning felt like more scraping than polishing. That&#x27;s usually a sign you&#x27;ve fallen behind, not that something&#x27;s wrong with you. A cleaning for a patient who&#x27;s fallen behind on care is naturally more involved than a standard maintenance visit. That&#x27;s fine, that&#x27;s what the visit is for.

Here&#x27;s the honest version: your hygiene visit interval should match your mouth, not a calendar habit from childhood. Smokers, patients with a history of gum disease, and folks managing conditions like diabetes often need to come in more often. If you&#x27;re not sure where you land, we&#x27;ll tell you plainly at your next visit, no guessing required.
