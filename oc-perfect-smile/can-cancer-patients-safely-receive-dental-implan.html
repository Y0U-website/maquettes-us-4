Which Types of Cancer Require Extra Attention?

From a dental implant perspective, patients who have been treated for head and neck cancers often require the most careful evaluation, especially when radiation involved the upper or lower jaw.

Radiation can affect the bone, blood vessels, soft tissue, and salivary glands in the treatment area. When the jawbone has been exposed to significant radiation, its ability to heal and remodel normally may be reduced.

These changes can remain important even many years after cancer treatment has ended.

This does not mean that someone who has received head and neck radiation can never have dental implants. Some patients may still be candidates. However, the location and amount of radiation, condition of the jawbone, time since treatment, and overall health must all be carefully reviewed before surgery is considered.

Cancers in other parts of the body—including breast cancer, prostate cancer, lung cancer, kidney cancer, thyroid cancer, multiple myeloma, and cancers that have spread to the bones—may also affect implant treatment.

In these cases, the concern is sometimes not the cancer itself but the medications used to treat it .

Radiation Therapy and Dental Implants

One of the most important questions we ask cancer survivors considering dental implants is:

“Have you ever received radiation to your head, neck, or jaw?”

Radiation therapy is designed to destroy cancer cells, but healthy tissues within the treatment area can also be affected.

If the jawbone receives significant radiation, its blood supply and ability to heal may change. This can increase the risk of complications following procedures such as tooth extraction, bone grafting, or implant surgery.

One of the more serious complications associated with radiation to the jaw is osteoradionecrosis , a condition in which irradiated bone becomes damaged and may have difficulty healing properly.

For this reason, even if your radiation treatment happened years ago, it is important to tell your dentist about it before undergoing oral surgery.

If you have recently been diagnosed with a head or neck cancer and have not yet started radiation, having a comprehensive dental examination beforehand can also be very important.

Chemotherapy and Dental Implant Treatment

Chemotherapy brings a different set of considerations.

Some chemotherapy treatments temporarily weaken the immune system or reduce white blood cell and platelet levels. This can make it more difficult for the body to fight infection or heal properly after surgery.

Because dental implant placement is an elective surgical procedure, it is generally not something that should automatically be scheduled during active chemotherapy.

Instead, timing should be coordinated carefully with the patient’s oncology team.

Once chemotherapy has been completed and the immune system, blood counts, and overall health have recovered, dental implants may become an option for many patients.

There is no single waiting period that applies to everyone.

Some patients may be ready sooner than others, which is why the decision should be based on your individual medical history and, when necessary, communication between your dentist and oncologist.

Cancer Medications and the Jawbone

Your medication history is especially important when considering dental implants.

Some patients with cancers that involve or have spread to the bones are treated with medications designed to slow bone breakdown and reduce complications from bone metastases.

These may include certain bisphosphonates, denosumab, antiresorptive medications, or other cancer therapies that affect bone healing .

While these medications can play an important role in cancer treatment, some are associated with a rare but serious condition called medication-related osteonecrosis of the jaw, or MRONJ .

The risk can be particularly important when certain medications are given at the higher doses used for cancer treatment.

For patients actively receiving high-dose antiresorptive therapy for cancer, elective implant surgery may not be recommended.

This is one of the reasons we always ask patients to bring a complete list of their current and previous medications.

You may feel completely healthy and may have finished chemotherapy, but a medication you are still receiving could significantly change the way we approach dental implant surgery.

Never stop or change a cancer medication because you are considering dental treatment. Any changes should always be discussed with your treating medical team.

What About Breast Cancer or Prostate Cancer?

Having had breast cancer or prostate cancer does not automatically prevent you from receiving dental implants .

Two people can have the same type of cancer but completely different implant considerations.

For example, one patient may have been treated years ago with surgery and have no current medical concerns. Another patient with the same type of cancer may have had bone involvement and may still be receiving medications that affect bone healing.

That is why your individual treatment history matters much more than the name of the cancer alone.

What Do We Evaluate Before Considering an Implant?

  • Current medications
  • History of radiation therapy
  • Location of any previous radiation
  • Jawbone quality and quantity
  • Gum health
  • Signs of infection
  • Healing capacity
  • Overall medical condition
