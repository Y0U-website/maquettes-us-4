Replacing Several Missing Teeth

When several adjacent teeth are missing, it is often unnecessary to place one implant per tooth. A smaller number of implants can support a bridge that spans the gap, which keeps the restoration fixed in place without relying on the remaining natural teeth for support.

The number and position of implants depends on where the gap sits in the mouth, how much bone is available, and how the bite loads that area during chewing. This is planned before treatment rather than decided in the chair.

Full-Arch Dental Implants

Full-arch treatment, often referred to as All-on-X, is designed for an entire upper or lower arch. Typically four to six implants are positioned in areas of the jaw with the strongest available bone, and they support one fixed arch of teeth.

The result is a restoration that stays in the mouth. There is no removable plate, no adhesive, and no nightly soaking. For patients who have struggled with dentures that move while eating or speaking, that stability is usually the deciding factor.

Treatment is digitally planned using 3D imaging so implant position, bite, and smile design are worked out in advance. Many qualifying patients can leave with a fixed provisional smile on treatment day, with the final restoration delivered once healing is complete.

Single Implants vs Full Arch: How the Options Compare

There is no universally better option. A patient with one missing molar does not benefit from full-arch treatment, and a patient with a failing upper arch is rarely well served by replacing teeth one at a time.

  • Single implant — one individual tooth is missing and the surrounding teeth remain healthy.
  • Multiple implant restoration — several teeth are missing and a fixed bridge on implants replaces them.
  • Full arch — many teeth across an arch are missing, severely damaged, or failing, and the arch is rebuilt as one restoration.

Bone Health and 3D Imaging

Implants need bone to hold them. A cone beam 3D scan shows bone volume and density, the position of the sinus, and the path of the nerve, which is why the scan comes before any recommendation.

Patients who have been told they have bone loss are not automatically disqualified from implant treatment. Depending on the findings, grafting may build the site up, or implants may be angled to engage denser bone elsewhere in the jaw. The scan is what makes that determination possible.

What the Treatment Timeline Looks Like

Single-tooth cases are often shorter and simpler. Full-arch cases involve more planning and more appointments, but many patients are surprised that the surgical portion is completed in one visit per arch.

  • Consultation and discussion of goals, history, and concerns
  • 3D imaging and evaluation of bone, bite, and remaining teeth
  • A personalized treatment plan with written costs and timing
  • Implant placement, with sedation options available
  • A healing period while the implants integrate
  • Delivery of the final restoration, followed by routine maintenance

Am I a Candidate?

Most reasonably healthy adults are candidates for some form of implant treatment. The evaluation looks at oral health and gum condition, bone volume, medical history, medications, smoking, and what you want the result to do for you.

Gum health is a particular focus. Untreated periodontal disease is one of the more common reasons implants fail, so it is addressed before implants are placed. If treatment makes you anxious, sedation dentistry options can be part of the plan.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I know whether I need a single implant or full-arch treatment?

It depends on how many teeth are missing or failing and on the condition of what remains. If one tooth is gone and its neighbors are healthy, a single implant is usually the appropriate option. If most of an arch is missing or the remaining teeth are not restorable, full-arch treatment is generally more predictable than replacing teeth individually. A 3D evaluation and clinical exam are what settle the question.

Are dental implants painful?

Placement is performed under local anesthetic, and sedation options are available for patients who want to be more deeply relaxed. Most patients describe mild soreness for a few days afterward, managed with routine post-operative care and a short period of softer foods. Discomfort varies with the extent of treatment and individual circumstances.

How long do dental implants last?

Implants themselves are designed to last many years, and with good hygiene and regular maintenance many function for decades. The restoration attached to them — a crown or a full-arch prosthesis — is the component more likely to need refreshing over time depending on material and wear. Gum health, bite forces, smoking, and routine care all affect longevity.
