1. Rinse with warm salt water

Mix half a teaspoon of salt into a cup of warm water and swish gently for 30 seconds. It cleans the area, soothes irritated gums, and can dislodge trapped food — a surprisingly common cause of sudden pain.

2. Floss around the painful tooth

A popcorn hull or seed wedged below the gumline can mimic a serious toothache. Gently floss both sides of the sore tooth before assuming the worst.

3. Use over-the-counter pain relief — the right way

For most adults, alternating ibuprofen (Advil) and acetaminophen (Tylenol) per the label directions controls dental pain better than either one alone. Ibuprofen also reduces the inflammation that’s usually driving the pain.

Never place an aspirin directly on the tooth or gums — it doesn’t work and it chemically burns the tissue.

4. Cold compress for swelling

If your face is swollen, hold a cold pack wrapped in a towel against your cheek for 15–20 minutes at a time. Avoid heat on the outside of your face — it can draw an infection outward.

5. Avoid the triggers

  • Skip very hot, cold, sweet, or acidic food and drinks.
  • Chew on the other side of your mouth.
  • Sleep with your head slightly elevated — lying flat increases blood pressure in the tooth and makes throbbing worse at night.

6. Call a dentist — sooner, not later

Pain relief is a bridge, not a fix. Whatever caused the pain (a cavity, a crack, an infection) is still there and progressing. Persistent tooth pain almost always means the nerve of the tooth is inflamed or infected, and that doesn’t heal on its own. Here’s how to handle it the smart way.

Understand what lasting pain usually means

  • Throbbing pain that wakes you up at night usually points to an inflamed or dying nerve (pulpitis).
  • Pain when biting down suggests a crack, a failing filling, or an abscess at the root tip.
  • Lingering sensitivity to hot (more than 30 seconds) is a classic sign of nerve damage.
  • Pain that suddenly disappears can actually be bad news — it may mean the nerve has died. The infection is still there and often returns as swelling.

Do this while you wait for your appointment

  • Stay on a consistent pain-relief schedule. Alternating ibuprofen and acetaminophen per label directions works better than chasing the pain after it spikes.
  • Keep the area clean. Brush and floss normally and rinse with warm salt water after meals. A cleaner site means less inflammation.
  • Don’t self-treat with leftover antibiotics. The wrong antibiotic at the wrong dose can mask symptoms while the infection spreads — and makes it harder to treat properly.
  • Track your symptoms. Note what triggers the pain, how long it lingers, and whether anything has changed. Those details help us diagnose the cause in minutes.

So… what actually counts as a dental emergency?

A dental emergency is generally any problem involving significant pain, swelling, infection, bleeding, trauma, or damage to a tooth or dental restoration that needs prompt attention. Some emergencies are obvious. Others are less clear — you may not be doubled over in pain, but a cracked tooth, increasing swelling, or a lost crown can still become a much bigger problem if it’s ignored. Here are the most common reasons to contact an emergency dentist.

Severe or persistent tooth pain

A toothache is one of the most common dental emergencies. Mild sensitivity that disappears quickly may not require urgent treatment. But pain that is severe, keeps returning, wakes you up at night, makes it difficult to eat, or continues to worsen deserves attention. Tooth pain can have several causes, including:

Pain is your body’s way of telling you something needs attention — you don’t have to simply "tough it out" until Monday. If your tooth is hurting significantly, give us a call and we can help you determine the next step.

  • A deep cavity
  • A cracked tooth
  • An irritated or infected nerve
  • An abscess
  • Gum infection
  • Trauma to the tooth

Swelling in your gums, jaw, or face

Swelling is something we take seriously, especially when it develops quickly or is accompanied by significant pain. It may be caused by an infection or abscess around a tooth or in the gums. Contact a dentist promptly if you notice:

Seek emergency medical care immediately if swelling is rapidly spreading or you’re having difficulty breathing or swallowing. Otherwise, call us as soon as possible — dental infections usually don’t resolve simply by waiting.

  • Swelling around a tooth
  • A swollen or tender gum
  • A lump or bump on the gums
  • Jaw or facial swelling
  • Pain combined with swelling

A broken or cracked tooth

A cracked tooth can range from a small chip to a significant fracture extending deeper into the tooth. Even when the pain is manageable, a broken tooth may expose sensitive internal structures and become more vulnerable to further damage. Contact a dentist if:

The sooner we evaluate the tooth, the better the chance of preventing the damage from becoming more extensive.

  • A large piece of the tooth breaks off
  • The tooth hurts when you bite
  • The tooth becomes sensitive to hot or cold
  • The broken area feels sharp
  • The tooth is bleeding, or you can see a visible crack
  • The tooth was damaged during an accident

A knocked-out permanent tooth

A knocked-out permanent tooth is a true dental emergency — time matters. If possible, pick the tooth up by the crown (the white portion you normally see) and avoid touching the root. If it’s dirty, gently rinse it without scrubbing, then contact a dentist immediately. In some situations a permanent tooth can be successfully placed back into the socket, especially when treatment happens quickly. If this happens during a weekend sporting event, accident, or fall, call us right away — and see our full step-by-step guide for a knocked-out tooth .

A tooth knocked loose or out of position

Sometimes a tooth isn’t completely knocked out but has been pushed sideways, backward, forward, or deeper into the socket. This also needs prompt evaluation. Don’t repeatedly push or wiggle the tooth — call an emergency dentist and explain what happened. Dental trauma can affect the tooth, surrounding bone, gums, and nerve even when the tooth still appears to be in place.

A dental abscess or suspected infection

An abscess is an infection that can develop around a tooth or within the gums. Possible warning signs include:

An abscess should not be ignored. Even if the pressure or pain temporarily improves, the underlying source of the infection may still be present. We can evaluate the area and determine what treatment is necessary to address the cause.

  • Severe or throbbing tooth pain
  • Swelling and tender gums
  • A pimple-like bump on the gums
  • A bad taste in your mouth or drainage
  • Pain when chewing or increased sensitivity
  • Fever or feeling unwell

A lost crown

Losing a crown may not always feel like an emergency, but it’s worth calling about. Without the crown, the underlying tooth can be sensitive, painful, vulnerable to breaking, and difficult to chew on. Save the crown if you still have it and bring it with you — depending on the condition of the crown and tooth, we can determine whether it can be reattached or whether another treatment is needed.

A lost or broken filling

A missing filling can expose a sensitive portion of the tooth. You may notice a hole or rough area, sensitivity to cold, pain when chewing, food repeatedly getting stuck, or a sharp edge. It’s tempting to ignore a missing filling if it isn’t painful, but the tooth can continue to weaken. Call us and we can help you decide how quickly it should be repaired.
