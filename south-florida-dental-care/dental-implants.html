What Can Dental Implants Replace?

One Missing Tooth

A single implant and custom crown can replace one missing tooth without requiring crowns on the adjacent teeth. This may be particularly valuable when the neighboring teeth are healthy and would not otherwise require restoration.

The final crown is designed according to the available space, gum contours, neighboring teeth, bite forces, and visibility within the smile.

  • Replacing a missing front tooth
  • Replacing a missing back tooth

Several Missing Teeth

When several neighboring teeth are missing, implants may support a fixed bridge. Because the bridge is supported by implants, it may not be necessary to place one implant for every missing tooth.

The appropriate number and position of implants depend on the location and length of the space, available bone, bite forces, and design of the final restoration.

All or Most Teeth in an Arch

Patients missing all or most upper or lower teeth may be candidates for an implant-supported or implant-retained denture. Implants can improve stability and reduce movement compared with a conventional removable denture.

Depending on the patient’s anatomy and goals, the final teeth may be removable by the patient or fixed in place and removable only by a dental professional.

  • Upper-arch tooth replacement
  • Lower-arch tooth replacement
  • Full, partial, and implant-supported dentures

Potential Benefits of Dental Implants

Dental implants have several potential advantages, but their suitability depends on the individual patient and treatment plan.

Implants do not completely prevent future bone changes, and they are not immune to infection, inflammation, mechanical wear, or failure. Long-term success depends on proper planning, healthy tissues, effective home care, appropriate bite management, and regular professional maintenance.

  • Replacing a missing tooth without preparing healthy neighboring teeth for crowns
  • Providing fixed support for a crown or bridge
  • Improving the stability of a removable denture
  • Restoring a more complete biting and chewing surface
  • Helping preserve bone in the area where the implant is placed
  • Creating a replacement tooth designed to complement the surrounding smile
  • Supporting several replacement teeth with strategically positioned implants

Are You a Candidate for Dental Implants?

Age alone does not determine whether someone is a candidate for implants. General health, oral health, available bone, healing capacity, and ability to maintain the restoration are often more important.

Some health conditions or risk factors do not automatically rule out implant treatment, but they may affect planning, healing, risks, or prognosis. Your dentist may request additional medical information or communicate with your physician when appropriate.

  • The number and location of missing teeth
  • The condition of the gums and remaining teeth
  • The height, width, and quality of the available jawbone
  • Your bite and the forces placed on the proposed implant
  • Tooth grinding or clenching
  • Diabetes and how well it is controlled
  • Tobacco and nicotine use
  • Medications and conditions that may affect bone or healing
  • Previous radiation or surgery involving the jaws
  • Your ability to clean around the implant restoration
  • Your expectations regarding treatment time and the final result

Why Gum and Bone Health Matter

A dental implant requires adequate bone for support and healthy gum tissue around the final restoration. If bone has been lost after an extraction or because of periodontal disease, grafting may be considered before or during implant placement.

Bone grafting is not required in every case. Whether it is appropriate depends on the location and extent of the deficiency, the proposed implant position, and the design of the final restoration.

Active gum disease should also be addressed before implant treatment. Patients with a history of periodontitis may still be candidates, but they require careful evaluation and consistent maintenance because they may face an increased risk of inflammation and bone loss around implants.

What to Expect During Dental Implant Treatment

Implant treatment usually involves several phases. The exact sequence and timeline depend on whether a tooth must be removed, whether grafting is needed, the number of implants, the healing response, and the type of final restoration.

1. Consultation and Diagnosis

Your dentist reviews your dental and medical history and evaluates your teeth, gums, bite, and missing-tooth area. Dental X-rays and, when appropriate, three-dimensional imaging may be used to assess bone and surrounding anatomical structures.

2. Restorative Planning

The final tooth or prosthesis should be considered before selecting the implant position. Implant placement must support a restoration that fits the available space, functions with the bite, can be cleaned, and looks appropriate within the smile.

3. Preliminary Treatment

Some patients require an extraction, treatment of gum disease, bone grafting, or other preliminary care. A temporary tooth or denture may be discussed when appropriate, particularly when a visible tooth is being replaced.

4. Implant Placement

The implant is surgically placed in the planned position. A local anesthetic is normally used. Post-treatment instructions may include temporary dietary changes, oral-hygiene guidance, medication instructions, and activity limitations.

5. Healing and Integration

During healing, bone forms a close connection with the implant surface in a process called osseointegration. This process usually takes time and varies according to the implant site, bone quality, treatment performed, health factors, and individual healing response.

6. Final Restoration

After the implant has achieved adequate stability, an abutment and a final crown, bridge, or denture are fabricated. The restoration is evaluated for fit, bite, appearance, and access for cleaning.

Some patients may qualify for a different or accelerated sequence, but immediate placement or immediate teeth are not appropriate for every situation.

Dental Implant or Dental Bridge?

Implants and traditional bridges can both replace a missing tooth, but they use different forms of support.

An implant may be worth considering when:

  • The neighboring teeth are healthy and do not need crowns
  • There is sufficient bone, and grafting is feasible
  • You prefer a restoration supported independently of adjacent teeth
  • You are comfortable with implant surgery and the required healing period

A traditional bridge may be worth considering when:

Neither option is automatically better for every patient. Read more about dental bridges and the factors involved in choosing between a bridge and an implant.

  • The neighboring teeth already need crowns or substantial restoration
  • You prefer to avoid implant surgery
  • Medical or anatomical factors make implant treatment less suitable
  • You want a fixed option without waiting for implant integration

Implant-Supported Denture or Conventional Denture?

A conventional denture rests on the gums and is removable. An implant-retained or implant-supported denture connects to implants for additional stability.

Implants may reduce movement and improve confidence during chewing and speaking, but implant treatment involves surgery, additional treatment time, higher initial cost, and ongoing maintenance of both the implants and prosthesis.

A conventional denture may remain appropriate for patients who prefer a nonsurgical approach or are not candidates for implant treatment. The right choice depends on anatomy, health, goals, dexterity, maintenance ability, and financial considerations.

Risks and Limitations of Dental Implants

  • Infection or delayed healing
  • Failure of the implant to integrate with the bone
  • Inflammation or bone loss around the implant
  • Gum recession or aesthetic changes
  • Injury to nearby anatomical structures
  • Loosening or wear of restorative components
  • Chipping, fracture, or wear of the crown or prosthesis
  • Problems related to grinding, clenching, or excessive bite forces
  • The need for future repair, component replacement, or additional treatment

Caring for Dental Implants

  • Brush thoroughly at least twice daily
  • Clean around and beneath the restoration as instructed
  • Use floss, interdental brushes, or a water flosser when recommended
  • Attend regular dental examinations and professional maintenance
  • Avoid tobacco and nicotine products
  • Wear a nightguard if recommended for grinding or clenching
  • Report bleeding, swelling, movement, discomfort, or bite changes promptly
