Dental implant treatment paths and timing

Not everyone follows the same schedule. These are common pathways, not fixed timelines or promises of eligibility.

Extraction, then healing

The site heals before an implant is placed. This staged approach may be recommended when the area needs time to recover.

Extraction with bone grafting

A graft may be placed to preserve or rebuild support, followed by healing and later implant placement.

Immediate implant placement

In selected situations, an implant may be placed when the tooth is removed. Examination and imaging determine whether this approach is appropriate.

Integration and restoration

After placement, the implant integrates with the bone before receiving its final crown, bridge, or denture. A temporary tooth may be possible in some cases.

Who may be a candidate for dental implants?

An implant evaluation may be useful if you have one missing tooth, a badly damaged or nonrestorable tooth, several missing teeth, a failed bridge, loose or uncomfortable dentures, or interest in fixed full-arch tooth replacement.

Candidacy depends on an examination, imaging, gum health, bone support, medical history, bite forces, medications, and other individual factors. These details also help determine whether periodontal care or another preparatory service is appropriate.

What we evaluate

  • Condition of the tooth and surrounding gums
  • Bone volume and implant location
  • Number of teeth being replaced
  • Bite, hygiene, and maintenance needs
  • Medical and dental history
  • Goals for fixed or removable teeth

Types of dental implant treatment

The implant fixture is the part in the jawbone, the abutment is the connector, and the restoration is the visible crown, bridge, or denture.

Single-tooth implant and crown

One implant fixture can support an individual crown without using the neighboring teeth as anchors. The crown is the visible part of the replacement tooth.

Implant-supported bridge

Strategically placed implants can support a bridge that replaces several teeth in a row. Fewer implants than missing teeth may be used, depending on the plan.

Implant-retained or implant-supported denture

Implants can improve the stability and retention of a denture. Some designs remain removable for cleaning, while other implant-supported restorations are fixed.

Full-arch and All-on-4® treatment

A full arch of replacement teeth may be supported by a planned number of implants. All-on-4® is one full-arch approach offered by HVDG, but anatomy, health, and restorative needs determine the appropriate design.

  • Several adjacent teeth — dental implants may support a bridge across the missing-tooth area.
  • Most or all teeth in an arch — full-arch implant options such as All-on-4® or All-on-6® may be considered.

Dental implants, bridges, and dentures

No single option is best for every patient. This overview can help you prepare questions for an individualized evaluation. For a closer look at the single-tooth decision, read dental implant vs. bridge .

Dental implant

  • Usually fixed
  • Does not require preparing neighboring teeth
  • Transfers chewing forces to the jawbone
  • Requires surgery, planning, and healing
  • Can replace one tooth or support multiple teeth

Traditional bridge

  • Fixed in place
  • Supported by prepared neighboring teeth
  • Does not replace the missing tooth root
  • Often completed without implant surgery
  • Commonly replaces one or several adjacent teeth

Removable partial denture

  • Removed for cleaning
  • May use nearby teeth for support or retention
  • Does not replace missing tooth roots
  • Can replace teeth in different areas
  • Fit and maintenance needs can change over time

Full denture

  • Conventional versions are removable
  • Replaces a complete arch of teeth
  • Rests on gum tissue without implant support
  • Requires daily removal and cleaning
  • Implants may be considered to improve retention
