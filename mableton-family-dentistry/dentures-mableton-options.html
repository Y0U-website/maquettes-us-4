Dentures In Mableton: Full, Partial and Implant-Supported Options

People asking about dentures in Mableton usually arrive with a story rather than a question. A few teeth have been lost over the years, a partial has stopped fitting, or a set made a decade ago clicks whenever they talk. They want to know what the options are now and whether anything holds better than adhesive.

There is more range than most patients expect, and the right answer depends on how many teeth are left and how much bone is there to work with.

Partial Dentures When Some Teeth Remain

If you still have healthy teeth in an arch, keeping them is almost always the goal. A partial denture fills the gaps and uses your remaining teeth for support and stability.

Partials restore chewing on both sides and keep the remaining teeth from drifting into open spaces, which is a slow problem that eventually changes your whole bite. They come out for cleaning, and the teeth that anchor them still need daily attention, the clasp areas collect plaque quickly, so those teeth need more care rather than less.

For a single missing tooth, a dental bridge or an implant is usually the better fit. Partials make the most sense when several teeth are missing in different parts of the arch.

Full Dentures When An Arch Is Finished

When no teeth remain in an arch, a complete denture restores the shape of your face, your ability to chew, and how you speak. An upper denture holds well by suction across the palate. A lower denture has less surface to work with, which is why the lower one is the more common complaint.

The fitting process is a series of appointments, not a single visit, impressions, a bite record, a try-in where you see the shape and color before anything is finalized, and then delivery with adjustments over the following weeks. Those adjustments are expected and included in the plan. Our comparison of complete and partial dentures goes further into the differences.

Implant-Supported Dentures For A More Stable Fit

If a loose lower denture is the problem, this is the option worth understanding. A small number of dental implants are placed in the jaw, and the denture snaps onto them.

The difference is stability. The denture stays put while you eat, adhesive becomes unnecessary or minimal, and because implants stimulate the bone, the ridge holds its shape better over time than it does under a conventional denture. Our post on implant-supported dentures explains how the attachments work.

The requirements are the same as for any implant: healthy gums and enough bone, which sometimes means grafting first. It costs more than a conventional denture and takes months rather than weeks. For patients who have spent years avoiding certain foods in public, that tradeoff is often worth it.

Adjusting To New Dentures

The first few weeks take patience. Expect more saliva than usual at first, some sore spots that we adjust, and speech that needs practice. Reading aloud for a few minutes a day genuinely speeds that up.

Start with softer foods cut small, chew on both sides at once to keep the denture balanced, and add tougher textures back gradually. Sore spots are not something to endure, call us and we will relieve the area, because trying to tough it out creates ulcers. We collected more tips for adjusting to new dentures from patients who have been through it.

Caring For Dentures Day To Day

Clean them over a folded towel or a sink of water, since a drop onto porcelain is how most dentures break. Brush them daily with a denture brush and a non-abrasive cleaner, not regular toothpaste, which scratches the acrylic.

Take them out at night unless we have told you otherwise, and store them in water or a soaking solution so the material does not dry and warp. Brush your gums, tongue and palate every morning too.

Keep coming in for exams even if you have no natural teeth left. We check the fit, look at the tissue underneath and screen for oral cancer. Our post on when to reline or replace dentures covers the signs to watch for.
