The short answer

A standard single dental implant at Palm Beach Institute of Dentistry in West Palm Beach is $2,400 . That price includes the implant, the abutment and the crown — the three parts that together replace one tooth from root to surface.

It is not a starting price, and it is not the implant alone with the rest quoted later. Those three components are what most people mean when they say a dental implant, and all three are in the number.

What the $2,400 covers

A dental implant is three separate pieces, and understanding them is the key to reading any quote you are given here or anywhere else.

Quotes that look unusually low often cover the first item only. If you are comparing prices between practices, the single most useful question you can ask is: does this figure include the abutment and the crown? If the answer is no, you are not comparing the same thing.

  • The implant. A small titanium post placed into the jawbone, where it takes the place of the natural tooth root. Over the following months the bone grows around it and locks it in place.
  • The abutment. A connector that screws into the implant and sits just above the gum line. It is the link between the part in the bone and the part you can see.
  • The crown. The visible tooth, made to match the shade and shape of the teeth beside it.

What the difference should not come down to is the implant itself. If you are quoted a low price anywhere, ask which implant system is being used and where the crown is being made. Those are fair questions and any practice should answer them plainly.

What can add to the price

Some mouths need preparatory work before an implant can go in. When that is the case it is quoted separately and explained at the consultation, before anything begins. The three most common are:

None of these are upsells invented at the chair. They are visible on a 3D scan before treatment is planned, which is why the consultation includes one. You will know what your case needs before you commit to anything.

  • Extraction. If the failing tooth is still in place, it has to come out first. Sometimes the implant can be placed during the same visit; sometimes the site needs time to heal.
  • Bone graft. Jawbone shrinks once a tooth has been missing for a while. A graft rebuilds enough volume to hold an implant securely. This is the most common reason a straightforward case becomes a two-stage one.
  • Sinus lift. In the upper back jaw the sinus floor sometimes sits too low to leave room for an implant. A sinus lift creates that room. We have a full explanation of the procedure here .

What dental insurance actually pays

This is where expectations and reality part company most often, so it is worth being blunt.

Many dental plans exclude implants entirely, treating them as elective. Plans that do contribute are usually capped by an annual maximum frequently in the region of $1,000 to $2,000 which is the ceiling on everything the plan pays that year, cleanings and fillings included. A plan may also cover the crown while excluding the surgical placement, or the reverse.

The practical result is that insurance rarely changes the decision. It more often takes a bite out of the total than it does the deciding. Our team will check your specific plan and tell you what it will and will not do before you start but go in expecting to fund most of an implant yourself, and treat anything the plan contributes as a reduction rather than a foundation.

If you have no dental coverage at all, our in-house membership plan is usually a better fit than buying an individual policy, which typically imposes waiting periods before major work is covered anyway.

Ways to spread the cost

Monthly payment options are available, and the practical question at the consultation is not whether you can pay $2,400 today but what monthly figure works for you. It is worth asking about financing even if you expect to pay outright, simply to see the options side by side.

One more thing worth timing: if your plan has an annual maximum you have not used, and your treatment can reasonably be staged across a year end, that is two annual maximums instead of one. Ask about it it is not always possible, but nobody will raise it if you do not.

Is an implant worth it against a bridge or a denture?

A bridge costs less up front. It also requires cutting down the two healthy teeth on either side of the gap to carry it, and it does nothing for the bone underneath, which continues to shrink. Bridges are typically replaced after a decade or so, and each replacement asks more of those neighbouring teeth.

A denture costs least and asks nothing of the surrounding teeth, but it rests on the gums, moves while eating, and accelerates the same bone loss.

An implant is the only one of the three that replaces the root as well as the tooth, which is what keeps the bone stimulated and the neighbouring teeth untouched. Whether that justifies the difference depends on how long you plan to keep the result and how much the bone matters in your particular case which is a conversation, not a formula.
