What is a dental implant?

  • 1 Implant fixture A small post placed in the jawbone. This is the part that replaces the tooth root and later supports the restoration.
  • 2 Abutment / connection The piece that connects the implant to the tooth you see. The exact connection is chosen to match the implant and the planned crown, bridge, or prosthesis.
  • 3 Crown, bridge, or prosthesis The visible tooth or teeth. This may be a single crown, a bridge on implants, or an implant-supported denture or fixed full-arch restoration.

What can implants replace?

Implants can replace one tooth or help rebuild a larger span of missing teeth. These are overview options. The right approach depends on your bone, gums, bite, and the teeth being replaced.

Single missing tooth

One implant can support a crown when a single tooth is missing or cannot be saved. Neighboring teeth do not have to be prepared for a bridge if the site is suitable.

Several missing teeth

Two or more implants can support individual crowns or an implant-supported bridge when several teeth are missing in a row. The number of implants is planned from the span, bite, and bone.

Implant-supported denture

Implants can stabilize a denture so it rests on fixtures rather than gums alone. This can improve retention compared with a conventional denture, while remaining a removable prosthesis in many designs.

Full-arch fixed teeth

When most or all teeth in an arch need replacement, a fixed prosthesis can be supported by several implants. The number and position of implants are planned for your anatomy and the restoration, not a one-size protocol.

Why robotic guidance?

Yomi S is an FDA-cleared computerized robotic navigational system for dental implant surgery. It is a planning and guidance tool. It is not an autonomous surgeon.

Yomi S helps Dr. Regina follow the planned implant position with guided control during surgery. She remains in control and performs the procedure. The robot does not place the implant on its own.

Published clinical studies of robot-assisted implant surgery across robotic systems have reported mean platform and apex deviations generally under 1 mm, with angular deviations around 1.6–1.7°. These measurements describe how closely placement matched the digital plan; they do not predict healing or guarantee long-term implant success.

  • 1 Haptic guidance Physical guidance in the robotic arm helps Dr. Regina follow the planned position, angle, and depth while she places the implant.
  • 2 Real-time tracking The system tracks movement during surgery so guidance can stay aligned with the plan if the patient shifts.
  • 3 Access and visibility The arm is designed to keep the treatment site reachable and in view while Dr. Regina works.
  • 4 Chairside controls Touchscreen, voice, and workflow tools support chairside use without changing who performs the surgery.

Who is a candidate?

Candidacy is decided after a clinical exam and 3D imaging. Implant candidacy depends on the whole picture—your oral health, 3D anatomy, medical history, bite, and restorative goals.

Limited bone does not automatically mean implants are impossible. Some patients need bone grafting, a sinus lift, staged treatment, or a different implant and restorative approach. Others are better served by saving a tooth with root canal treatment, or by a conventional bridge or denture.

Active gum disease, untreated infection, poorly controlled medical conditions, or a bite that would overload a new implant may mean treatment is delayed or planned in stages.

  • A missing tooth, a failing tooth, or several teeth that cannot be restored predictably
  • Bone volume and density at the planned site
  • Gum health and oral hygiene
  • Medical history, including diabetes control and medications that affect healing
  • Smoking
  • Bite forces and grinding or clenching
  • Space for the implant and the final restoration
  • Your goals for appearance, chewing, and how many visits you can commit to
  • When a tooth may still be saved with root canal treatment
  • Full-mouth rehabilitation for more complex needs

Bone grafting and sinus lift

An implant needs enough bone to hold a stable position and support the tooth that will sit on it. After an extraction, or when a tooth has been missing for some time, bone can be limited in width or height, or close to the sinus.

Bone grafting can rebuild volume before an implant is placed, or it can be done at the same time as implant placement when the remaining bone can still stabilize the fixture. A sinus lift is a specific form of grafting in the upper back jaw, where the sinus often sits close to the molars.

These procedures are planned only when the scan shows they are needed. They are not added to every implant case.

Can an implant be placed the same day as extraction?

In selected cases, an implant can be placed at the time a failing tooth is removed. That decision is made from the conditions in the site, not from a same-day promise.

Immediate placement depends on infection, remaining bone, whether the implant can be stable at placement, gum and esthetic conditions, the bite, and the restorative plan. If the socket is not suitable, the site may be grafted and allowed to heal first.

A temporary tooth may sometimes be possible. Same-day implant placement does not always mean the final crown is delivered that day. Immediate placement and immediate loading are separate decisions, and neither is guaranteed.

How we plan implant surgery

CBCT / 3D imaging

A cone beam scan shows bone volume, sinus anatomy, nerve position, and the space available for a tooth. This is the starting point for a prosthetically driven plan.

Clinical and restorative evaluation

Dr. Regina examines the gums, bite, remaining teeth, and the tooth or teeth being replaced. The goal of the implant is a working, cleanable restoration—not only a fixture in bone.

Digital implant planning

The planned implant position, angle, and depth are set in 3D with the final crown, bridge, or prosthesis in mind. Adjacent teeth and the opposing bite are part of that plan.
