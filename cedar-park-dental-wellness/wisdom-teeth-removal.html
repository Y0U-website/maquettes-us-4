Do all wisdom teeth need to be removed?

Monitoring may be appropriate

Pain or recurrent inflammation

Pain, swelling, drainage, bad taste, or repeated inflammation around a partially erupted tooth should be evaluated.

Decay or gum disease

A hard-to-clean wisdom tooth can develop decay or contribute to gum disease even when it is not currently painful.

Neighboring teeth or pathology

Removal may be recommended when findings include damage or risk to the second molar, a cyst or other pathology, or anatomy that cannot be kept healthy.

Signs your wisdom teeth should be evaluated

Schedule an evaluation for pain, pressure, swelling, drainage, a bad taste, recurrent gum inflammation, difficulty cleaning behind the second molar, or a cavity near a wisdom tooth. An examination may also find gum disease, damage to a neighboring tooth, or pathology such as a cyst before it causes obvious symptoms.

Erupted, partially impacted, and impacted wisdom teeth

Fully erupted

The entire crown is visible in the mouth. A fully erupted wisdom tooth may function and remain healthy, or it may develop decay or gum problems because of its location.

Partially impacted

Part of the tooth has emerged while gum tissue or bone still covers another part. The area can be difficult to clean and may develop recurrent inflammation or decay.

Fully impacted

The tooth remains beneath gum tissue or bone. Position, root anatomy, surrounding structures, symptoms, and signs of disease determine whether removal, continued monitoring, or referral is appropriate.

Sedation and anxiety management

Local anesthesia is used to numb the treatment area. Depending on medical history, procedure, and patient needs, options may also include nitrous oxide, oral sedation, or IV sedation. Dr. Regina provides IV sedation for appropriate patients and procedures. Learn more about sedation options .

What recovery commonly looks like

Swelling, soreness, limited jaw opening, and light bleeding can occur after removal. Your instructions may include cold packs, soft foods, activity limits, careful oral hygiene, and medications taken exactly as directed. Avoid smoking or vaping, straws, vigorous rinsing, and other activities for the period specified by the clinical team.

These general points do not replace the postoperative instructions for your procedure. Healing varies with the number and position of teeth, complexity, health, and individual response.

Risks and when to contact us

Possible issues include pain, swelling, bleeding, dry socket, infection, delayed healing, damage to nearby teeth or structures, and temporary or, rarely, persistent altered sensation when nerves are close to a treatment site. Risk depends on anatomy and complexity and is discussed before treatment.

Contact Cedar Park Dental Wellness for significant or worsening pain or swelling, fever, drainage, persistent bleeding, medication concerns, or other symptoms identified in your instructions. For uncontrolled bleeding, difficulty breathing or swallowing, or another potentially life-threatening symptom, seek urgent medical care. What happens during wisdom tooth treatment

Examination and imaging

Dr. Regina reviews symptoms, medical and dental history, eruption status, gum health, neighboring teeth, and available X-rays. A CBCT may be recommended when three-dimensional detail will affect planning.

Complexity and scope assessment

Tooth position, root development, surrounding bone, access, and relevant anatomy are reviewed. Dr. Regina explains whether treatment can be performed in the office or whether referral is appropriate.

Anesthesia and sedation plan

Local anesthesia is used for the treatment area. Nitrous oxide, oral sedation, or IV sedation may be considered based on health history, procedure complexity, and patient needs.

Tooth removal

An erupted tooth may be removed directly. An impacted tooth may require an incision, controlled removal of overlying bone, or division of the tooth into sections. The specific steps depend on anatomy.

Site management

The site is cleaned and managed based on the procedure. Sutures may be used when Dr. Regina determines they are appropriate, and PRF or PRP may be placed as an adjunct in selected cases.

Instructions and follow-up

You receive instructions for bleeding, swelling, diet, activity, oral hygiene, medications, and when to call. Follow-up is arranged when indicated.

Comfort and sedation options

Local anesthesia is used for the treatment area. Nitrous oxide, oral sedation, and IV sedation are available when appropriate. Medical history, medications, procedure complexity, and patient needs determine which options can be considered.
