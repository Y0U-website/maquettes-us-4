Oral Cancer Exam (Prevention)

Approximately 75 % of oral cancers are linked to modifiable risk factors. Patients who smoke or use tobacco heavily are encouraged to speak with Dr. Diaz about cessation support — reducing or eliminating these behaviors meaningfully lowers long-term risk.

  • Excessive alcohol consumption — heavy or chronic alcohol use is one of the strongest independent risk factors for oral cancer, particularly cancers of the tongue, floor of the mouth, and throat.
  • HPV infection — human papillomavirus, particularly HPV-16, is now a leading driver of oropharyngeal cancers and is responsible for a growing proportion of cases in patients with no history of tobacco or alcohol use.
  • Prolonged sun exposure — lip cancers are strongly associated with cumulative UV exposure, making outdoor workers and those who spend significant time in the sun higher-risk candidates.
  • Age and gender — oral cancer rates increase with age and have historically been more common in men, though incidence among women and younger adults has risen significantly due to HPV-related cases.
  • Prior oral cancer diagnosis — patients who have previously had oral cancer are at elevated risk of recurrence or new primary lesions and require more frequent monitoring.

What the Exam Involves

Visual and Manual Examination

Dr. Diaz and our team visually examine the lips, tongue, floor of the mouth, cheeks, gums, palate, and the oropharyngeal region at the back of the throat. The face and neck are also evaluated, and the lymph nodes and salivary glands are palpated to detect any unusual swelling or firmness. Patients are asked to extend the tongue and move it side to side so the lateral borders — a common site for squamous cell carcinoma — can be examined thoroughly.

The following findings are evaluated with particular attention:

  • Red patches (erythroplakia) — red patches on the floor of the mouth, the sides of the tongue, or the soft palate that do not resolve within two weeks can be indicative of pathologic changes.
  • White patches (leukoplakia) — hardened white or gray slightly raised lesions that appear anywhere inside the mouth. Leukoplakia has a variable malignant potential and warrants further evaluation.
  • Ulcerations and non-healing sores — sores that bleed easily and do not heal within 10 to 14 days are a significant clinical finding.
  • Lumps and tissue thickening — unexplained firmness, asymmetry, or thickening of tissue in the oral cavity or neck that was not present previously.

VizLite™ Plus Technology

For patients at elevated risk, or when the visual exam identifies an area warranting closer inspection, Dr. Diaz uses VizLite™ Plus — a chemiluminescent light technology that allows abnormal tissue changes to be identified that would be invisible to the naked eye.

The VizLite™ exam adds only a few minutes to the appointment and involves no discomfort, needles, or tissue disruption. If any abnormality is detected, Dr. Diaz will discuss next steps, which may include monitoring over a follow-up period, referral for a biopsy, or coordination with a specialist depending on the clinical picture.

What Happens If Something Is Found

Finding an abnormality during an oral cancer exam does not mean oral cancer is present. Many lesions have benign explanations, including trauma, infection, and inflammatory conditions. What it means is that the finding warrants a clinical response — typically a follow-up exam in two to four weeks to assess whether the lesion has resolved, or a biopsy to determine its nature definitively.

If oral cancer is confirmed, early-stage localized disease is highly treatable. Treatment typically involves surgical excision, radiation, or a combination depending on the extent and location of the lesion. Dr. Diaz will coordinate referrals to the appropriate specialist and support you through every step of that process. Our goal at every visit is to ensure that if something is present, it is found at the stage where outcomes are best.
