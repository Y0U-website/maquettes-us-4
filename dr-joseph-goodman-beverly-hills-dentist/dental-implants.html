How do implants compare to bridges and dentures?

All three replace missing teeth, but only the implant replaces the root. An implant stands on its own in the bone. A bridge leans on the two teeth beside the gap, which means reshaping them. A denture rests on the gums and comes out to clean. The root is why an implant protects the jawbone while the other two do not.

Only the implant keeps the jawbone working, since chewing through the post stimulates the bone the way a real root would. A bridge asks two healthy teeth to carry the load, and the Cleveland Clinic notes bridges often need replacing after five to seven years. A denture costs least up front, yet it loosens and does nothing for bone loss. The best pick depends on your mouth and budget.

How does the dental implant process work?

The process runs in clear stages over three to six months. First, Dr. Goodman examines your mouth, takes 3D images, and plans the implant down to the crown. He places the titanium post, then lets it fuse with the bone. Once it heals, he attaches the custom crown you see when you smile. You wear a temporary tooth in between, so no gap ever shows.

One dentist handling both parts changes the result. Because Dr. Goodman plans the crown from day one, the final tooth fits the plan instead of getting bolted on later. Healing takes three to six months while the bone bonds to the post, and a thin jaw gets a graft first. When the bone is ready, the crown goes on.

Who is a good candidate for dental implants?

You are a good candidate for a dental implant when you have healthy gums, enough jawbone to hold the post, and general health steady enough to heal. Most adults qualify. The main requirement is bone, since the implant fuses to it over three to six months. Dr. Goodman confirms your fit with a 3D scan before any work begins.

A few things raise the risk, and none of them means an automatic no. Thin bone can be rebuilt with a graft. Controlled diabetes and treated gum disease still allow strong results. Smoking and unmanaged health conditions lower success, so Dr. Goodman addresses those first. If an implant is the wrong move for you, he says so and points you to a better option. You get an honest answer, not a hard sell.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do dental implants hurt?

Most patients feel little during placement and only mild soreness for a day or two after. Dr. Goodman numbs the area fully, so the surgery itself is comfortable, and over-the-counter relief handles what follows. About 60 percent of his patients deal with dental anxiety, so comfort gets planned from the start. For nervous patients, an in-house anesthesiologist, Dr. Thomas Einstein, handles deeper sedation, and no one rushes you or judges the fear.

What if I do not have enough bone for an implant?

This is common, and it rarely ends the plan. When the jaw is thin or soft, a bone graft rebuilds support, acting as a scaffold your own bone grows into over a few months. Dr. Goodman checks your bone with 3D imaging and tells you up front if a graft is needed. Research on implants placed in grafted bone reports survival on par with natural bone, per a study in the National Library of Medicine .

Can I get implants if I have diabetes, an autoimmune condition, or low bone density?

Often yes, though each needs a closer look. With controlled diabetes, implants succeed at rates close to healthy patients, while poorly controlled blood sugar raises the risk. Autoimmune conditions and low bone density call for the same careful planning, not an automatic no. A study of medically managed patients found about 96 percent implant survival at 10 years when conditions were controlled first. Dr. Goodman reviews your full health history before he plans the work.

How long do dental implants last, and can one fail?

With good care, implants last decades. More than 9 in 10 still work at 10 years, and the post can serve for life while the crown may need replacing sooner. Failure is uncommon, and when it happens it usually traces to gum infection or bone loss, not the implant itself. Long-term research puts 10-year survival as high as 95 percent. Brush, floss around it, keep your cleanings, and skip tobacco.

Is a single tooth implant worth it, or is a bridge better?

Both replace one tooth, but differently. A bridge caps the two teeth beside the gap, grinding down healthy teeth, and tends to need replacing in five to seven years. An implant stands alone, leaves the neighbors untouched, and protects the bone under the gap. The Cleveland Clinic notes bridges are harder to clean and do not last as long. A bridge is faster and lower cost up front, so Dr. Goodman weighs both for your tooth and budget.

What is the downside of dental implants?

The main downsides are cost, time, and minor surgical risk. An implant costs more up front than a bridge or denture, and healing runs three to six months. Infection, nerve irritation, or sinus issues are possible but uncommon in healthy patients. The Mayo Clinic notes most complications stay minor and treatable. Dr. Goodman screens your health and 3D scans first to keep your risk low.
