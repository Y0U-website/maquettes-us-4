Are You a Candidate for Dental Implants?

You may be a candidate for dental implants if you have one or more missing teeth and want a stable replacement supported by the jawbone. Candidacy depends on your oral health, general health, available bone, and ability to maintain the restoration.

Patients with bone loss or gum disease are not automatically excluded from implant treatment. Additional procedures may be recommended to create a healthier and more stable treatment site.

Dental implants also require a healing period and continued maintenance. Your dentist will explain the expected sequence so you can decide whether the process fits your needs.

  • The number and location of missing teeth
  • The height and width of available jawbone
  • The health of your gums
  • Your overall health and medical history
  • Medications that may affect treatment or healing
  • Tobacco or nicotine use
  • Tooth-grinding or clenching habits
  • Your daily oral hygiene
  • Your treatment goals and expectations

What to Expect During Implant Treatment

Dental implant treatment usually occurs in stages. The overall timeline varies according to the location of the missing tooth, bone quality, healing, and any preparatory treatment that may be necessary.

The treatment area is examined and planned using appropriate dental imaging. A damaged tooth may need to be removed, and some patients may require bone grafting or other preparatory care before implant placement.

The implant post is surgically positioned in the jawbone. Local anesthesia is used during placement, and additional comfort options may be discussed when appropriate.

Once healing is complete, an abutment is connected to the implant. A custom crown, bridge, or denture is then designed and attached to restore the missing tooth or teeth.

Your bite and the fit of the restoration will be checked. Follow-up appointments allow the team to monitor the implant, surrounding gums, and final prosthesis.

Dental Implants Compared With Traditional Bridges

An implant-supported crown and a traditional dental bridge can both replace a missing tooth, but they receive support in different ways. Your dentist can help you compare the procedures, timelines, maintenance needs, and condition of nearby teeth.

Dental Implant and Tooth-Replacement Options

Dental implant treatment can be adapted to different patterns of tooth loss. Non-implant options may also be appropriate depending on your oral health, preferences, and budget.

A single implant can support a custom crown without requiring the neighboring teeth to serve as anchors. The final crown is shaped to coordinate with the surrounding teeth.

An implant-supported bridge can replace several adjacent missing teeth. The bridge receives support from strategically positioned implants rather than requiring one implant for every missing tooth.

Implants may be used to improve the support and stability of a full or partial denture. The restoration may be removable or fixed depending on its design.

A dental bridge offers a fixed alternative that uses nearby teeth for support. It may be considered when implant treatment is not preferred or appropriate.

Full and partial dentures provide removable options for replacing several teeth or a complete arch. They do not require an implant for every missing tooth.

Recovery and Dental Implant Care

Tenderness, swelling, minor bleeding, or bruising may occur after implant placement. Follow the instructions provided by your dental team regarding eating, cleaning the area, medications, and activity.

The implant should not be placed under excessive pressure while the surrounding bone and gum tissue heal. Your dentist will explain when and how you can resume normal chewing in the treated area.

  • Keep the treatment area clean as instructed
  • Eat foods recommended for the healing stage
  • Avoid disturbing the implant site
  • Take prescribed or recommended medications as directed
  • Avoid tobacco and nicotine products
  • Attend all scheduled follow-up visits

Caring for the Final Restoration

Dental implants cannot develop cavities, but the gum tissue and bone surrounding them can become inflamed or infected. Daily cleaning remains essential.

Regular examinations allow the dentist to monitor the implant, restoration, gums, bone, and bite.

  • Persistent or increasing pain
  • Swelling that worsens instead of improving
  • Bleeding or drainage around the implant
  • Gum recession near the restoration
  • A loose implant crown, bridge, or denture
  • A change in the way your teeth meet

How Much Do Dental Implants Cost?

The cost of dental implant treatment depends on the number of implants, the type of final restoration, the location of the missing teeth, and the condition of the treatment area.

Extractions, bone grafting, dental imaging, temporary tooth replacements, abutments, crowns, bridges, or dentures may affect the total cost. A single implant crown and a full-arch restoration involve different treatment plans and fees.

Dental insurance benefits vary and may cover certain parts of the procedure rather than the entire treatment. Our team can help you review available benefits and discuss the practice’s financial options .
